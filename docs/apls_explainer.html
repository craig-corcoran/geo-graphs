APLS, Pair by Pair

APLS · Average Path Length Similarity

APLS scores a road graph by the travel distances it supports.

APLS picks two points, measures how far you have to travel between them in the ground truth, measures the same trip in the prediction, and scores the difference. It never compares a predicted road to a real one. Every other property of the metric follows from that choice. This page draws the actual routes, on real tiles, from the same code path that produces the reported number.

The mechanism

One pair, measured twice

Start with a source graph and a target graph. Walk every edge of the source and drop a control point every 50 m. These are the endpoints APLS will use, and nothing about them depends on the target. Take two of them. The shortest path between them through the source is la.

Now find those same two ground positions in the target graph. Each one snaps to the nearest point on the nearest target edge, provided that edge comes within 25 m. The shortest path between the snapped points is lb. The pair scores

1 − min(1, |la − lb| / la)

and the direction’s score is the mean over every pair. A missing route scores zero. The metric has no graded penalty short of that.

Consequences

Four things that follow

Two directions, because one is gameable

Predict roads everywhere and every real trip survives: gt → prop stays high. Predict almost nothing and the few control points you do place match exactly: prop → gt stays high. The reported APLS is the harmonic mean of the two, which is dragged toward the worse one, so neither trick pays.

A missing route scores zero

If the target has no path between the snapped points (or no edge within 25 m of a control point to snap to at all), there is no lb to subtract. The metric does not guess one. Partial credit here would hide the failure the metric exists to catch.

One severed road poisons a whole tile

Control points are dense and pairs are drawn across the entire network, so a single break that splits the graph in two zeroes every pair with one endpoint on each side. Split n points evenly and k(n−k) of the n(n−1)/2 pairs cross the break: with 66 control points, 1089 of 2145 pairs (just over half) go to zero at once.

The penalty is asymmetric

The denominator is la, the source length. A route that comes back half as long keeps 0.50; one that comes back twice as long keeps 0.00, and anything longer is clamped there. Detours are punished harder than shortcuts, and a long enough detour is indistinguishable from no road at all.

What the score does not say. A high APLS says travel distances agree at the sampled pairs. It does not say the geometry is right: a road drawn 20 m off its true line still snaps within the 25 m limit and still measures the same length. That is deliberate, since a road network is judged by the trips it supports. It also means APLS and a pixel metric can disagree in both directions without either being wrong.
