Roads from pixels, and the metric that tells you when you failed

A segmentation model turns satellite imagery into a road mask. Skeletonization turns the mask into a graph. The interesting part is that a mask which still looks almost perfect can produce a graph that is badly, unrecoverably wrong — and pixel accuracy will not tell you.

The problem

Two metrics that disagree

IoU compares pixels: what fraction of the road surface did you find. APLS compares routes: it samples points across the network and asks whether the travel distance between them survived. One is what a segmentation loss optimizes. The other is what a road network is for.

Drag the slider. It punches small gaps into a perfect mask — the scale of a tree shadow, an overpass, a truck in a lane — then rebuilds the graph and rescores it.

The model

What was built

A U-Net predicts a road mask from three-band pan-sharpened imagery. Morphological thinning traces the mask into a graph: pixels with two neighbours are interior, three or more are a junction. Douglas–Peucker simplification, spur pruning and junction snapping clean it up. APLS scores the result against OpenStreetMap-style ground truth.

Every chip is scored against its own ceiling — the APLS the same pipeline reaches from a perfect mask on that chip. That number is not constant. It runs near 0.98 on a flat downtown grid and drops sharply where roads cross without meeting.

Held-out result

Per-chip spread

On comparability. Published SpaceNet APLS figures run roughly 0.63–0.74. This sits inside that range, and the metric agrees with the reference implementation to 3×10−5. But it is one AOI — Las Vegas, the easiest, a regular grid in high desert contrast — scored on a private 20% holdout rather than the official test split, where published numbers span four cities including Shanghai and Khartoum. In range on the easiest city is the honest claim; matching published results is not.

What broke

Four bugs the invariants caught

Each was silent: the pipeline ran, produced plausible geometry, and reported a believable number. Each was found by asserting a property that had to hold — not by looking at output.

apls(G, G)
0.935 → 1.000

Polyline orientation

An undirected graph reports each edge in whichever orientation its adjacency dict stores, so an edge read back as (u, v) is not guaranteed to start at u. Three call sites assumed it did and built mirrored geometry. Caught by the one invariant that cannot be argued with: a graph scored against itself must be exactly 1.0.

31 of 62
pixels dropped

Parallel edges silently discarded

Tracing visits every edge from both ends, so the second visit must be recognized and dropped. Keying that on endpoints plus path length collides whenever two genuinely distinct routes share both — two arcs of a loop — and threw one away along with its geometry. It needed single-pixel junctions to trigger, which is why thick-road masks never showed it.

ceiling
0.245 → 0.982

Labels that were never noded

SpaceNet ships each road as one line running straight through its intersections, so crossing streets share no vertex. Built as given, one chip is 33 edges across 30 disconnected components. The mask joins what the labels leave apart, so the metric calls every junction invented. Without noding, every model number would have been measured against a ceiling of 0.25.

0.243 × 0.300 m
per pixel

Square in degrees, not on the ground

The imagery ships in a geographic CRS. A Vegas pixel is 0.243 m east–west and 0.300 m north–south — square in degrees and 19% out of square in reality — while the whole pipeline measures distance in pixels and calls it metres. Reprojection to UTM is now mandatory, and a geographic CRS is refused outright.

The ceiling

Why a perfect mask still fails

Render the ground truth to a flawless mask, trace it back, and score it. It does not return 1.0. That is not a tuning failure — it is a property of the representation.

A 2D binary mask cannot express a road passing over another. Where a bridge crosses a street, the labels correctly record no junction: you cannot turn from one to the other. Flattened to a plane, the two roads touch, and skeletonization has no choice but to emit a junction that does not exist.

Sweeping road width from 1.5 to 18 m and resolution from 0.5 to 2 m/px moves the ceiling only between 0.861 and 0.901. Thinner roads and a finer grid do not close it, because the problem is not resolution. The loss lands entirely in the direction that punishes invented connections, which is the signature an overpass leaves.

This is the measured argument for a different representation — per-cell directional edge slots, which can encode two roads meeting at one pixel without a junction. A mask simply has nowhere to put that fact.
